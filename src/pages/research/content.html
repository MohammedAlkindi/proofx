GB-002 · Controlled Goldbach selection

Which structural rules actually help?

All seven policies receive the same allocation within each band of 100 even integers. Three intervals, three budgets, and four family removals test whether the full priority policy earns its complexity.

Reference
3,000 even integers in three fixed intervals
Budgets
20, 50, or 100 evaluations per interval
Primary analysis
Exact rational expected recall and variance
Simulation check
2,048 seeds × 7 policies × 3 intervals

The target is the lowest 5% of partition-count ratios within each band, including ties. Uniform ordering inside a policy's priority groups makes expected recall exactly calculable. Simulation intervals measure Monte Carlo error on these fixed inputs; they do not estimate performance on larger integers.

Primary result

What the study found

The full priority policy has higher expected recall than uniform and ascending selection in all nine interval/budget combinations. At budget 100, its expected recall is 12.90%, 20.64%, and 20.68%, compared with uniform's 10% in each interval.

The twice-prime rule hurts this objective. Removing it raises expected recall to 33.04%, 34.46%, and 28.71% at budget 100, and improves every tested combination. Removing the modulo-6 rule changes no selections because earlier groups already fill the quotas. An inactive ablation says nothing about that rule outside this ordering and budget.

This revised policy is different from GB-001's generator. Narrow bands match size allocation but leave possible within-band size effects. The study measures reference-query selection quality, not runtime, mathematical progress, or generalization. Selecting a better policy from these results requires a new prospective study on other domains.

How the evidence is checked

The verifier recounts every reference and replays every seed. A separate implementation enumerates prime pairs using trial division and checks exact moments through inclusion-indicator covariances. All 54 simultaneous Monte Carlo intervals contain the exact contrasts. Source snapshots, environment versions, all 43,008 policy-replicate records, and all 30 stratum thresholds are in the bundle. Checksums identify files; they do not authenticate authorship or establish peer review. The protocol was committed before outcomes, without an external preregistration service.

GB-001 · Goldbach partition selection

Finding small observed-to-expected partition ratios

Does ProofX's structural candidate selection recover more low-ratio integers than uniform sampling or an ascending scan, given the same evaluation budget?

Domain
Even integers 4–10,000
Budget
64 distinct candidates / run
Seeds
0, 1, 2
Metric
Recall of the bottom 5% of ratios

For each integer, G(n) counts unordered prime pairs, including equal primes. The ratio is G(n) / H(n), where H(n) is the leading unordered Hardy–Littlewood approximation. An exhaustive reference defines the lowest 5%, including boundary ties. Policies select candidates without reading that reference.

Interpretation limits

In this published run, directed selection finds 19, 12, and 19 low-ratio inputs across the three seeds; uniform sampling finds 8, 4, and 4. The ascending control finds 26 in each row. Directed selection therefore does not beat both baselines here. The ascending control concentrates on small integers, where the approximation has the largest finite-size error; this result motivates comparisons within held-out size intervals.

The approximation has finite-size error, especially for small integers. Finding low ratios measures alignment with this chosen heuristic; it does not measure progress toward a counterexample. The policies visit different size distributions. Repeating this protocol on prespecified held-out intervals is necessary before arguing that a pattern generalizes.

The verifier independently checks every exact count and witness, hashes, domain, budget, and summary. It does not authenticate authorship or certify timings. Rerun the recorded policies and compare ledger hashes to check policy provenance.
